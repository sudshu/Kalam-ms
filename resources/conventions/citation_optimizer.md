# Citation Maximization Strategy Guide

This document outlines strategies applied at every stage of manuscript writing to maximize the paper's citation potential. These principles are embedded in each skill but collected here as a reference.

---

## 1. Title Optimization

**Goal**: A title that is searched for, clicked on, and remembered.

- **Include high-volume keywords**: Use terms that researchers actively search for (e.g., "methane emissions", "satellite remote sensing", "CO2 growth rate" rather than obscure jargon)
- **Be specific about the contribution**: "First satellite-based detection of..." or "A global inventory of..." — titles that promise something concrete get more clicks
- **Name the method if it's novel**: Method names become search terms (e.g., "TROPOMI-based", "physics-informed ML")
- **Avoid question titles**: Declarative titles citing specific findings get ~20% more citations than question titles
- **Avoid colon titles when possible**: Simple, direct titles are more memorable
- **Length**: 10-15 words is the sweet spot — long enough to be specific, short enough to be scannable

## 2. Abstract as Advertisement

**Goal**: An abstract that makes readers want to cite you.

- **Front-load significance**: The first sentence should hook the broadest possible audience
- **Include the "quotable claim"**: A crisp, self-contained statement that others can cite without reading the full paper
- **Be quantitative**: "reduced by 40% ± 5%" not "substantially reduced"
- **End with an implication**: The last sentence should point forward — this invites follow-up work that cites you
- **Keyword density**: Include all major search terms in the abstract (search engines weight the abstract heavily)
- **No jargon in sentence 1**: The opening must be accessible to adjacent fields

## 3. Figure Design for Reuse

**Goal**: Figures that get shared in talks, textbooks, and other papers.

- **Create a "hero figure"**: At least one schematic, framework diagram, or synthesis figure that captures the paper's concept. These are the most reused and cited figure type.
- **Standalone captions**: Each figure must be understandable from caption alone — readers scan figures first
- **Comparison tables**: Comprehensive comparison/benchmark tables are highly citable — researchers reference them when writing their own papers
- **Maps with clear context**: For spatial data, include geographic references, scale bars, and enough context for non-specialists
- **Consistent design language**: Use a cohesive color scheme and layout across figures — builds visual brand
- **High resolution**: Figures that look good in slides get shared more

## 4. Methods Documentation for Reproducibility Citations

**Goal**: Methods so clear that others cite your paper when they use your approach.

- **Step-by-step clarity**: Write methods as if teaching a graduate student to reproduce the work
- **Name your approach**: If the method is novel, give it a name (creates a citable term)
- **Parameter documentation**: List all model parameters, configurations, input datasets with versions
- **Validation protocol**: Document how you validated results — this protocol itself becomes citable
- **Code/data availability**: Papers with open code get more citations (others cite you when they use your code)
- **Flowcharts**: A methods flowchart is both a useful figure and a citable reference for the methodology

## 5. Strategic Self-Citation

**Goal**: Build on your own body of work without over-citing.

- **Reference your own prior papers** where they provide genuine methodological or contextual foundation
- **Typical range**: 3-7 self-citations is normal and expected; more than 10 may draw reviewer attention
- **Use self-citations to build narrative continuity**: "Building on our previous work (Author et al., 2024), we extend..."
- **Cite your most-cited papers**: This reinforces their visibility and creates a "cluster" of related work
- **Don't force it**: Every self-citation should be justifiable to a skeptical reviewer

## 6. Bridge References

**Goal**: Expand the communities that know about and cite your work.

- **Cite adjacent fields**: If writing about methane, cite relevant CO2, air quality, climate policy, and environmental justice papers
- **Cite methodological analogs**: If you use inverse modeling, cite inverse modeling papers in other domains (ocean carbon, air quality, nuclear monitoring)
- **Cite review papers**: Authors of review papers often reciprocate when they update their reviews
- **Cite emerging researchers**: Early-career researchers are building their reference lists — if you cite them, they're more likely to cite you back
- **Geographic diversity**: Cite work from different regions/institutions — broadens your citation base internationally

## 7. Positioning at Field Intersections

**Goal**: Frame the paper so multiple communities see it as "their" paper.

- **Introduction framing**: Open with a topic that spans fields (climate change, air quality, energy transition) before narrowing to your specific contribution
- **Discussion connections**: In Discussion, explicitly connect your findings to 2-3 different research communities
- **Keywords spanning fields**: Choose keywords that span fields (e.g., both "atmospheric remote sensing" AND "greenhouse gas policy")
- **Multiple "hooks"**: Different readers should find different reasons to cite you:
  - The remote sensing community cites your methodology
  - The carbon cycle community cites your CO2/CH4 results
  - The policy community cites your implications

## 8. Open Access & Discoverability

- **Open access journals** get ~18% more citations (Nature Communications, AGU Advances, ACP are all OA)
- **Preprints**: Posting on ESSOAr or arXiv before/during review gets early visibility
- **ORCID**: Ensure all authors have ORCID IDs linked — improves discoverability
- **Data repositories**: Data in Zenodo/Figshare with proper DOIs gets cited independently
- **Social media**: A well-crafted tweet/thread about the paper, especially with a compelling figure, amplifies reach

## 9. Post-Publication Amplification

After the paper is published:
- Share on Twitter/X with the hero figure and a 1-sentence takeaway
- Prepare a 1-paragraph summary for your institution's press office
- Email the paper to collaborators in adjacent fields
- Present the work at conferences within 6 months of publication
- Update Google Scholar and ORCID profiles
- Add to your institutional and group website publication lists
