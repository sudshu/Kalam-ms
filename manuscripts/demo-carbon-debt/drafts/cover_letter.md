Dear Editor,

We submit "Repeated hot-dry extremes leave a persistent carbon debt in modelled tropical
forests" for consideration as an Article in *Nature Communications*.

Recovery from climate extremes is routinely assessed from how quickly ecosystem carbon
fluxes return to their pre-event range, because fluxes are what the observing systems
resolve. Our manuscript shows that this diagnostic and the carbon stock can disagree for
two decades. In paired 20-year simulations at four tropical forest sites, net biome
production returns to baseline within 6–18 months after 80% of hot-dry events, yet 73%
of 800 site-parameter simulations end with less ecosystem carbon than the matched
counterfactual — a pooled median deficit of 1.9 Mg C ha^−1^. A factorial decomposition
attributes most of the deficit to productivity that never occurred rather than to
carbon actively respired away, which explains why a return-to-baseline flux diagnostic
cannot see it.

The result is relevant beyond carbon-cycle modelling. Recovery-time metrics are used to
judge ecosystem resilience, to attribute variability in the land sink, and increasingly
to argue for the durability of forest-based carbon removals. Our experiment identifies a
specific way those inferences can be optimistic, and specifies the additional
measurement — a stock comparison — needed to close the gap.

We are explicit about the limits. This is one model structure at four sites; the pooled
uncertainty interval spans zero, so we claim the sign of the effect rather than its
magnitude; and the counterfactual that makes the experiment possible is also what makes
it unobservable. We state these in the Discussion rather than leaving them for review.

The manuscript is about 1,400 words of main text with two display items and is not under
consideration elsewhere. We suggest reviewers with expertise in terrestrial carbon-cycle
modelling, drought legacy effects, and ecosystem resilience metrics.

Thank you for your consideration.
